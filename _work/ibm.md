I accepted an offer to join IBM's Cloud department in June 2019 after a close friend solicited me to jump on the phone with his boss, who was actively looking to hire developers for a primarily front-end software team. At the time I'd been intentionally purusing work that wasn't tied to a computer - after earning a living with programming for almost 10 years, it felt philosophically irresponsible that I hadn't explored other work experiences. When the IBM job fell in my lap, however, it felt too good to pass up.

My first project at IBM Cloud was Cloud Foundry Enterprise Edition (CFEE), which was used by enterprise clients to deliver their software both on IBM's infrastructure and private infrastrcture. I joined the project as the existing front-end team was transitioning away from the project, which left myself and a group of other mostly new-hires repsonsible for taking over the existing front-end interfaces, written primarily in React and Go. This transition created a vacuum of ownership that I was lucky to be able to step up and help fill.

The most interesting facet of working on CFEE was that even building front-end solutions still required a fundamental understanding of the software delivery infrastructure our product was designed to simplify. CFEE had been repackaged to run on top of Kubernetes, so it was necessary to learn the in's and out's of that platform in order to solve problems within our own. Joining IBM from a [small start-up](/work/roof.html) was essential in that I'd already been responsible for the delivery and maintenance of my own front-end software, and my years of experience [delivering websites for clients](/work/minicreative.html) didn't hurt either. I eventually became responsible for the CI/CD pipelines of many of our micro-services in addition to my responsibilities pushing bug fixes and features for these same services. 

My front-end team was eventually assigned a new product, IBM Cloud's Code Engine, which was ostensibly very similar in that we were still creating an offering that simplified the software delivery process for our users, only this time without the bulky layer of Cloud Foundry in between our offering and Kubernetes. Joining this project closer to the beginning allowed me to get involved with conversations surrounding project architecture, and a colleauge and I advocated heavily for the development of a documented, public-facing API that would also hold up both Code Engine's web interface and CLI, a component that had somehow not been prioritized in the original architecture of the offering. I was offered the task of prototyping this API in addition to my existing duties of building and maintaining Code Engine's CLI. I had full ownership of the Service Binding functionality of our CLI, and I was selected to be a speaker on this topic at IBM's [Cloud Native Day](https://events.bemyapp.com/cloudnativeday). I was also given the opportunity to mentor two college interns that joined our team over the summer.

CFEE eventually entered a maintenance phase, and as its developers were assigned new projects, someone still needed to solve problems for our remaining enterprise clients. Because I'd helped fill the expertise vacuum, and also demonstrated an ability to communicate technical issues to our clients, I filled the role of fixing issues and responding to the majority of incoming tickets during CFEE's maintenance phase. 

When I took the job at IBM, I'd made a personal commitment to keep the job no longer than a year: I was still serious about pursuing other forms of work. That year was up in June 2020 -- during the uncertain beginnings of the COVID pandemic -- and as many of my closest friends found themselves unemployed, the idea of leaving a fully-remote, fulfilling and rewarding job felt absurd. I stayed on for another year until COVID had mostly subsided and I felt ready to move to New Orleans and do [construction for Habitat for Humanity](/work/habitat.html).
